The Book That Was Left Out

4 min read · ages 3–6 · bedtime story

At closing time the lamps went off one at a time down the long table, until only the low green one on the desk was burning.

Miss Hall walked the last round with the trolley, and Biscuit the cat rode on top, because that was the best place.

Ada came too, because checking the tables was her job, and Ada did it very slowly, so as not to finish it.

On table seven was a book that wasn't on the trolley, lying face up, as though somebody had been called away.

"Somebody left that out," said Miss Hall. "It will find its shelf in the morning, and whoever borrowed it can have a polite letter."

Ada picked it up. It was heavy, and bigger than a book with stories in it, and the cloth was grey, and the spine said nothing.

In the back cover was a pocket with a card in it, a day written in blue pencil. "That's a long time ago," said Ada, reading it upside down.

"It will be in here somewhere, then," said Miss Hall, who had not looked. "Put it in the box. Biscuit, get down."

The book wouldn't go into the box. Ada tried twice, and in the end it lay on the trolley like a heavy whale.

So Ada opened it, and it fell open in the middle all by itself. A photograph slid out, face up.

It was a photograph of the reading room.

Ada didn't understand at first, because it looked exactly like the place she was standing: the tall windows, the long table, the low green lamp.

And there, on the middle of the carpet, a small girl in a coat sat holding a big grey book with nothing on the spine.

Ada looked at the book in her hands, and then at the picture, and then at the picture again.

"That's the same book," said Miss Hall. She looked at the picture for some time. "Well," she said. "It has been round."

"But it's in the picture of this room," said Ada. "With her in it, holding it. So it was on this carpet, and now it's here."

"Books do that," said Miss Hall. "That is the whole job of a book. It goes off somewhere and waits, and then it comes back."

They did not put it in the box. Miss Hall set it on the middle table, in the pool of the lamp, and laid the photograph back.

Then the big lights went off, all but the one on the desk, and the trolley went squeak, squeak, squeak. After that there were no sounds at all in the building.

Ada's mother came up the path with her coat open, and Ada told her about the small girl on the carpet the whole way home, twice.

And in the library, in the dark, the big grey book lay open at the photograph, with the little girl in the coat holding it. The room had come back into the book, and the book stayed in the room.

Ada was asleep before her mother had finished the second telling, and the lamp stayed on a little longer than it needed to.

THE END.

One question for afterwards: If somebody drew a picture of your bedroom, what would you want them to draw?

Did you know? The little pocket in the back of a library book, holding a card with the day it must be returned, was invented in 1905 at Newark Public Library. Almost every library in the world has copied it since.
