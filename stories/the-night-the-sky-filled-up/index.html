The Night the Sky Filled Up

5 min read · ages 3–6 · bedtime story

It was the middle of the night, and the house was asleep, and the moon was not out. The moon takes the night off now and then.

On this night it had things to do elsewhere. In the garden there was a shed, and on the shed there was a small grey cat called Nell, and Nell was not asleep.

She was looking at the sky. That was the whole of her plan, and there was nothing else in the garden worth looking at, except the sky, and tonight it had almost nothing in it.

One star. Two. Three.

The three of them sat up in the cold, not saying anything, and the rest of the sky was dark all the way down to the trees.

"That is not a sky," said Nell. "That is a sky with the rest taken off."

So she counted them properly, the way you count sheep, or steps, or anything else you need to get through. One, two, three, four.

Then she could not find the fifth. She looked to the left and the sky was empty, and she looked to the right and the sky was empty.

"They are going out," said Nell. "Every time I look, there is one fewer."

The stars held absolutely still.

She tried harder. She made her eyes into little holes, the way cats do when they are being extremely serious, and she counted again. One, two, three, exactly as before.

Not one more, and not one less either, which was a small comfort. On the gatepost there was an old owl, who had heard all of it.

"You are looking too hard," said the owl. "I count them. It is a full-time job, and it takes all night, and I have been doing it for a very long time."

"How many are there?" said Nell. The owl did not answer. That was a question about her job.

Nell wanted to know very much. So she sat on the shed roof and kept looking, and the sky stayed nearly empty.

Her eyes began to hurt a little. The wind came over the field and went through her fur and put it up in the wrong places.

At some point, without deciding to, she shut her eyes.

And behind her shut eyes there was the sound of the house going on without her. The fridge humming, the clock doing tick, and a long way off a dog who was also awake.

And while she was not looking, the sky got on with it. Stars came out behind the ridge, and then more stars, further along, quietly, in ones and twos.

It was the way lights come on in a street after everyone has gone to bed. It took a long time, and that is the good part.

Nell opened one eye, because she was a cat, and a cat cannot help it.

The sky was full.

The moon was out after all, sitting low and pale with a whole cold lake of her own light, and there were so many stars that Nell could not count them.

The four were all still there. And the fifth she had lost somewhere in the middle.

"The stars are very rude," said Nell. "They make you wait."

She had looked at the sky all night to find out what it was doing, and she had also spent all night letting the sky get on with it.

Then she stood up on the shed roof and stretched, and looked once more, and the sky was still full.

And it was turning, very slowly, the way it has turned since before anybody was alive, one star at a time, all the way round the pole star, which is the one that does not move.

There was no hurry about any of it. Nell came down off the shed and went to find somewhere warmer, and the garden went back to being a garden.

The light stayed exactly where it was, and so did the dark, and so did the house, ticking, with the garden in the middle of it and the sky on top.

And one small grey cat went slowly to sleep in between.

THE END.

One question for afterwards: Is there a window in your house where you can see the sky from your bed?

Did you know? It takes about twenty to thirty minutes for your eyes to get used to the dark. After that, stars that were there the whole time come out.
