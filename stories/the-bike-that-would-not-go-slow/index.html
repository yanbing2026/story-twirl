The Bike That Would Not Go Slow

5 min read · ages 3–6 · bedtime story

The bicycle had belonged to Grandad, and now it belonged to your child, which is a thing that happens to a great many bicycles.

It was a good bike. It was dark green, with a bell on the handlebars and a basket on the front and one slow squeak out of the back wheel.

your child's first proper ride was down the long hill on the green, which was the best part and the worst part.

The top of the hill was quiet. There were nettles at the top, and a gate, and a view of the whole town with all its little lights on. At the bottom there was a wall, a bench, and about nine metres of grass.

Grandad walked beside and held on to the back of the saddle. It was lovely. It was the loveliest thing that had ever happened to anybody.

And then the hill started to go.

A bicycle with nobody on the pedals is only a heavy thing on a slope, and this one let go like it had been waiting all afternoon to.

your child did not scream. That part comes afterwards. your child simply held on very hard to the handlebars and watched the grass come up to meet the front wheel.

It arrived.

The bike stopped, and the basket came off. Nothing was broken, and your child's heart was going, and Grandad said, quite calmly, "Right. Now the brakes."

"Both of them?" said your child.

"Both at once," said Grandad. "Squeeze the two together. That is the trick, that is."

So your child squeezed the two brakes together.

SHREEK.

It was the loudest noise the green had ever heard. The front wheel wobbled, and your child put both feet down into the grass and stopped the whole thing with your child's own knees.

your child sat there afterwards breathing very hard, while a blackbird explained what it thought about the entire business.

Grandad nodded slowly.

"Well," he said. "You are one who stops with both brakes."

And your child was not one who stopped with both brakes. That was not quite the right answer, and your child did not know it yet.

The trouble was that your child wanted to go down that hill again, which was perfectly reasonable and also the end of everything.

"Go on, then," said Grandad. "I'll walk down beside you."

But halfway down, something in your child thought: what happens if I stop pedalling?

your child took both feet off the pedals and held them up in the air.

The bike went on by itself. Slowly.

The air pushed back a little. The hill gave a little back. The basket stayed on. The whole thing came down the green as gently as a leaf on a pond, while your child's legs did absolutely nothing at all.

At the bottom, your child put one foot on the path.

And it stopped.

"There," said Grandad. "It had it in it the whole time: a freewheel, which lets the back wheel carry on turning once your feet stop. It was never the brakes. It was only ever speed."

your child went back up the hill and did it eleven more times, which took most of the evening, and every single one of them was a little slower than the one before.

By the time the sky had gone properly dark, going slowly had stopped feeling like something your child had to work at, and had started to feel like the best part of the whole day.

Grandad unhooked the basket and hung it on his own handlebars, and they walked home, and the one slow squeak out of the back wheel went tick, tick, tick, all the way down the lane.

THE END.

One question for afterwards: What is something at home you could do more slowly and it would still work perfectly well?

Did you know? The back wheel of a bicycle has a freewheel on it, a little ratchet that lets the wheel keep turning after your feet have stopped, which is why a bike coasts downhill all on its own.
