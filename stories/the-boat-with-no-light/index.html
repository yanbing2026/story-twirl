The Boat With No Light

4 min read · ages 3–6 · bedtime story

It was the last crossing of the day. The river had gone flat and smooth, and the sky was going from orange to blue.

Along the bank the lamps came on one at a time. click, click, click, as if somebody were turning them on.

Mr Ash waited in his boat with his oars. He had been in it for thirty-one years and had an opinion about most things. The only other passenger was Nell.

"Can I carry the lantern?" said Nell.

"It wants to stay in the boat," said Mr Ash. "Out on the bow it would fill with water, and I'd find my way home by ear."

So it hung in the middle, making a small yellow circle on the water. Mr Ash pushed off.

Halfway across, the wind got up in one soft push, and pfft, the lantern went out. It made no sound at all.

But it was the same boat, and the oars went plip, plip, plip.

"That's only the wind," said Mr Ash. "It's a friendly wind. Four minutes to the steps."

The far wall had a lamp on top, and it was how she knew which steps were hers. Now there was only dark, and dark is full of shapes that are all more or less the right shape.

Mr Ash stopped rowing, and the boat drifted on until it stopped too.

"Two sets of steps on that side," he said. "The big ones, and the little ones by the boathouse. With a light they look different. Without, they don't."

"I know this river like the back of my hand," said Mr Ash. "The hand isn't helping tonight."

"Can we listen?" said Nell. Mr Ash said nothing, which from Mr Ash was a great deal.

Nell put her head on one side. The water was soft on one side, and on the other it went over stones: hiss, hiss, hiss, like a finger along a comb.

"That's the bank," said Nell. "It's the stones by our steps. The steps make a different noise, and then it goes deep by the wall."

Nell nodded, in the dark, where nobody could see it. "I know that sound the way you know the stairs at home."

Mr Ash put the oars back in and rowed towards where she was listening. He never said she was right. He was not that sort of man.

The hissing came nearer, up over the side of the boat, and the wood knocked against something solid. click.

"Steps on the left," said Mr Ash. "You're quite right, Nell — sit down, I have to tie up, and that takes two hands."

He lit the lantern on the top step, and its yellow square lay on the water, and the water was pleased with it. They walked up together, the lantern swinging between them, and the river went back to being a big dark nothing.

At Nell's gate there was a cup of milk on the table, and the window upstairs was showing the moon. Nell thought about all that light coming such a long way to reach her face. Then she went to bed, and the story came down after her.

THE END.

One question for afterwards: When it is dark at home, what sound tells you that everything is all right?

Did you know? The moon makes no light of its own. Everything you see up there is sunlight bounced off grey dust, and it has to travel 384,400 kilometres to reach your eyes, which takes about a second and a quarter.
