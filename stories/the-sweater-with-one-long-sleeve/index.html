The Sweater with One Long Sleeve

4 min read · ages 3–6 · bedtime story

It was a cold Friday at the little house at the end of the lane. By eight o'clock the sky had gone the colour of a plum.

There was a parcel on the table with string round it, and it had been there since the morning. your child had spent all day trying not to look at it.

It was from Nan. your child knew that because of the string, which Nan always tied in the same way.

At the door Nan had said one thing. "One of them is longer. I counted on purpose. Leave it alone."

Inside the parcel was a soft green sweater with two rows of small buttons. your child put it on straight away, and your child's fingers went warm at once.

Then your child looked down at the two sleeves. One came down to the wrist, like a sleeve should.

The other one was much, much longer. It came past the knuckles and right down over the hand.

"That one is broken," said your child.

So your child pulled the long sleeve up so that the two of them would match. It slid straight back down, with a soft whoosh.

So your child tried it rolled up instead. Rolled up, it was a fat lump, and it came loose while the tea was brewing.

The cuff button shot off and went under the table. It was a quarter past six before your child found it.

An hour went past, and the sweater was no better.

After that your child sat down and looked at the sweater for a very long time.

The green wool had a small hole in it, and one loose thread, and the hem was a bit crooked, and it was still the best thing in the room.

Then your child put the sweater over the back of the chair, and left it sitting in the dark with its sleeves hanging down.

At bedtime the room went cold. It did that every night.

The cold came up through the floor first, and then sat at the end of the bed like a cat that would not be told.

your child got out of bed and picked the sweater up. Both hands went round the long sleeve.

"I did it on purpose," said your child out loud, just to hear how it sounded.

Then your child put the sweater on, and slid your child's right hand up inside the long sleeve, the way you slide a hand into a pocket.

And it was warm. Not hot. Warm the way a cup is warm.

The cold came off your child's hand and went away down the wool, all the way past the elbow.

your child stood there in the dark room with one hand in and one hand out, and said absolutely nothing at all.

Nan had not knitted a broken sleeve and got the arms to match. Nan had knitted a pocket.

The cold comes at night, and now the cold had somewhere to put your child's hand.

Nothing in the house was broken, your child decided, climbing back into bed.

It was only that a thing made for the night is a very hard thing to see in the daytime.

The long sleeve went along the pillow beside your child's face, warm as a paw. Outside, the wind went past the window and on.

your child's hand stayed warm, and the wool had a hole in it, and a loose thread, and it was not broken at all.

Goodnight, sweater.

THE END.

One question for afterwards: Somebody in your house has made something just for you. Do you know what it is for?

Did you know? Real knitters count on purpose. Lots of knitting patterns tell you to make one sleeve longer, so your hands can slip inside it and stay warm.
