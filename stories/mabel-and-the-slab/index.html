Mabel and the Slab

5 min read · ages 3–6 · bedtime story

Under the garden path it is always evening.

There is no light down there and no wind, and the earth is warm after a whole sunny day in it.

Mabel lay in the warm earth and moved her back a little, to see how it felt.

It felt excellent.

Past the fence and past the bean sticks was the compost heap, and the heap was where Mabel went when she wanted to be warm for a long time.

Tonight, Mabel wanted to be warm for a long time.

So she set off down her tunnel, which she knew well, because it was her tunnel.

She went past the roots of the beans, which tickled. She went past the flat grey stone that tasted of nothing, which was her favourite one.

And then she came to the slab.

It was a long grey thing that went right across her tunnel, and it was not going anywhere.

Mabel pushed it. She pushed it for a very long time, with her front end, and then with her back, which is the proper way to push.

The slab stayed exactly where it was.

So Uncle Tub came along. Uncle Tub was a mole, and Uncle Tub was very pleased about all of it.

"Going under, are you," said Uncle Tub. "Good, that is the way. Feel along it with your nose and keep on going. I have never been stopped by a slab."

So Mabel felt along it with her nose, and kept on going.

She went under a great deal of path, and past a fence post, and past a root the size of her arm. It was dark, and it was still going. It was a slab the whole way.

Mabel stopped. And she lay there in the dark, quite cross, and listened, which she had not bothered to do before.

Above her, the feet of children went past. Patter, patter. Their boots were saying things she could not hear.

Then she heard something else. Not above. Off to the left.

It was a sound like sugar being poured very slowly, and Mabel knew that sound well, because it was an old road.

Ants had made it, and beetles had made it, and the little white roots of a birch tree had woven through it and packed it soft.

So Mabel turned left. Down a bit, and round, and up a bit, and round again. It was the long way, and the wrong way, and the way that takes all night.

She went along it anyway, because the earth on the left was soft, and the earth on the right was hard.

By the time she was warm and tired, the beans were overhead, and then the sky, with a few stars in it, and a smell of old apples.

And then the earth opened.

Not much. Only at the end of her nose. Only enough to get her head out and have a look around.

Mabel was at the bottom of the compost heap, at the warm end, in the dark.

It was warm, and it smelled of everything that had ever been in that garden. Mabel lay back down into it until there was nothing of her left to look at.

Above her, the garden went to bed. The little lights went out in the houses, one by one.

The path stayed where it was. Nobody knew that under the middle of it there was a new tunnel, and there would still be one there in the morning.

Mabel did not have to hurry. That was the good part. She had the whole warm night, and she was right in the middle of it.

THE END.

One question for afterwards: What do you like to do while you are waiting for a slow thing to happen?
