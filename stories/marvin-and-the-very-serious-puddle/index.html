Marvin and the Very Serious Puddle

4 min read · ages 3–6 · bedtime story

After the rain, the whole pavement was covered in puddles. Small ones. Medium ones. And one enormous one, right outside Marvin's gate, shaped like Australia.

Marvin put on his boots. Marvin's boots were yellow, and they made a sound that Marvin liked very much: squelch.

"Marvin," said his mother. "If you jump in that puddle, you will be wet from your hat to your socks."

Marvin looked at the puddle. The puddle looked back, very calmly, the way a puddle does.

"I understand," said Marvin.

He was not lying. He understood completely. He understood that the puddle was enormous, that his boots were yellow, and that some things in life are simply meant to be jumped in.

So he jumped.

SPLASH.

It was a magnificent splash. It went up. It went sideways. It went into the hedge, where a blackbird was sitting, minding its own business. The blackbird made a noise like a rusty door and flew away.

Marvin stood in the middle of the puddle, wet from his hat to his socks, feeling extremely good.

And then the puddle spoke.

"Excuse me," said the puddle.

Marvin froze.

"I said excuse me," said the puddle. "I am working."

"Working?" said Marvin.

"Working," said the puddle. "I am an enormous puddle. Do you know how much sky I have to hold? I am holding the entire sky, upside down, all day. Clouds. Birds. One aeroplane, twice. It is a very big job and I am doing it very well, and then somebody in yellow boots comes along and jumps straight through the middle of my sky."

Marvin looked down. He could see the sky in the puddle, in pieces now, wobbling.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I didn't know you were busy."

"Nobody ever does," said the puddle, rather sadly. "Puddles look like nothing. That is exactly why we get jumped on."

Marvin stood still in the water and thought about being looked at and thought to be nothing. He did not like the way it felt.

"Can I help?" he said.

The puddle went quiet for a moment. "Well," it said. "You could stop standing in the middle of me."

Marvin stepped out, carefully, and stood on the dry bit by the gate.

Slowly, very slowly, the puddle got its sky back. A cloud came in. Then a whole tree, upside down. Then the sun, which was pleased to be included.

"Better," said the puddle. "Much better. Thank you."

And Marvin found that he was wet, and cold, and had a hole in one sock, and he did not mind one bit. He had made friends with a puddle, which is not a thing most people manage before breakfast.

The next morning, the puddle had gone somewhere to do its next job. But it had left something behind: a perfectly round, flat, dry circle of pavement, exactly the size of a puddle, and right in the middle of it, one small feather — the blackbird's, from the day before.

Marvin kept it in a tin.

And he never jumped in an enormous puddle again, unless he had first asked politely.

THE END.

One question for afterwards: The puddle was holding the sky. What is something that looks small but is actually doing a big job?

Did you know? A puddle holds the whole sky, upside down — every puddle is a mirror lying flat on the ground.
