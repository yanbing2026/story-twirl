The Shell That Was Not Broken

4 min read · ages 3–6 · bedtime story

The sea had gone out a long way that evening, so far that the water was a grey line along the edge of the sky.

The sky was pink at the top and orange underneath, and the pools in the sand were holding all of it.

Tam and Grandad walked the wet sand, looking for what the tide had brought in. The sea loses things and never once says sorry.

Tam picked things up and put them down again. Grandad carried a bag and said nothing, which kept the evening going.

Then Tam found a big one.

It was a shell the size of two hands, white going pink at the tip, with a long crack down the side and a piece gone from the lip.

Tam did not pick it up at once. Then, with two hands, very slowly, the way you pick up something that is asleep.

"We're taking this home," said Tam.

"Tam," Grandad said, without raising his voice. "Put it back down. Nothing lives in a broken shell."

"They want the good ones," said Grandad. "They need the good ones. That is the whole of it."

Tam did not put it down, and did not argue either. Tam just held it, and looked at the crack, and thought.

And then Tam heard something.

tap. tap.

It was not the sea, which was a long grey line away. It came from inside the shell, and it was very small, and very polite.

Tam knelt down in the wet sand, put the shell down gently, crack facing up, and waited.

Grandad started to say something, and Tam put one finger up, and Grandad stopped.

Nothing much happened for quite a while. Then the crack widened, and out came one claw, purple, with a cream stripe, testing the air.

Then the other one. Then a face, all eyes. And a soft pale body, not at all like a crab out of a picture.

And the crab turned round and backed into the shell and made itself at home, and the crack stayed exactly as it had come out.

It wasn't a broken shell. It was a door.

"Oh," said Grandad. "Oh, I see. I have been wrong about shells for a long time." He sat down beside Tam in the wet sand, which took a while.

Tam waited until the crab had stopped moving before standing up, because Tam had learned that things need a minute.

And further down the sand there were more shells: dozens of them, all on their sides, all with the same long crack. Some were moving.

It was nearly dark. They walked back up the beach with the shells still where they were, and the sea came in behind them, taking its time.

Grandad carried the bag home and never complained. At the door he said, "Next time you find one, you can have a proper look. Just don't be too keen."

Tam thought about that on the stairs. A thing can look completely finished and still have somebody living in it.

Then Tam was in bed with the window pushed up a bit, and the sea going on out there in the dark. The sound came, and went, and came again.

THE END.

One question for afterwards: Is there something you were a bit scared of that turned out to be something else?

Did you know? A hermit crab has no shell of its own. It borrows an empty snail shell, and when it has grown too big for it, it has to go and find a bigger one, so it always tries the hole that fits.
