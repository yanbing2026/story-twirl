The Kettle That Would Not Go Quiet

4 min read · ages 3–6 · bedtime story

The house was asleep, and the kitchen was not.

It was just past midnight. The oven light was on, and the window over the sink was black and shiny as a plate.

Dad was asleep in the chair by the table, with a cup of tea he had made before his shift. The tea had gone completely cold.

And the kettle on the hob would not stop.

Click, click, click. Off, then on. Then off, click, click, click, like a very small clock that had lost its way.

Wren came down in her socks, which she was not supposed to do, and stood in the doorway to listen.

"Dad," she said. "The kettle is clicking."

Dad opened one eye. "Switch it off at the plug, love. That'll do it."

Wren pulled the plug out. The flex hung down like a tired arm.

Click. Click. Click.

The kettle carried on regardless.

"Fault in the wiring," said Dad, putting his head back against the chair. "We'll ring the shop in the morning."

Wren stayed where she was. The little light on the kettle was still on, and in that dark kitchen it was the size of a full moon.

She walked over and looked at it for a long time.

"Dad," she said. "Has it got any water in it?"

Dad sat up. The chair complained about it. He lifted the lid and put his face right down over the inside of the kettle, which is what you have to do to see.

"Oh," said Dad.

The kettle was bone dry. He had boiled the last of the water before his shift and then put it back to remind himself to switch it off, and it had been trying to boil nothing ever since.

"Well," said Dad, in a voice that was working quite hard. "That is my fault."

Wren went to the cold tap and filled the kettle right to the top. The water was very loud in the quiet kitchen. It made her jump, and then she liked it.

She set it back down, pressed the button, and stood well back.

Click. The kettle woke up. A low hum, and a rattle underneath it, and then the whistle, going up and up like something climbing out of a hole.

Then, all on its own, it stopped.

Dad sat down again. "Well," he said. "It was thirsty."

And since the water was hot, and it was half past midnight, they made two mugs of hot chocolate in the good cups and sat in the dark kitchen and listened to the fridge humming underneath everything.

"Why does it go higher when the water's nearly gone?" Wren asked.

"Little swirls," said Dad. "Big lazy ones first. Then smaller, and faster, and faster, until they sound like somebody screaming."

Wren put her ear close to the kettle. It was cooling now: tick, tick, tick, slower, and slower, and quieter, a thing that had finished working, resting.

They carried the cups up the stairs in both hands, slowly, and nobody said anything, because there was nothing left to say and it was very late.

And from the top of the stairs you could still hear the kettle going tick, tick, tick, like a small heart remembering how to be slower.

THE END.

One question for afterwards: Which sound in your house would you still like to hear when everything else has gone quiet?

Did you know? A kettle whistles when tiny swirls of steam come off the water and beat against the inside of the spout. Cambridge engineers only worked that out in 2013, more than a hundred years after people had started asking.
