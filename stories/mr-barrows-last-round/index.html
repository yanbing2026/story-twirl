Mr. Barrow's Last Round

5 min read · ages 3–6 · bedtime story

At five past eight the lamps were on all along Mill Row, and Mr. Barrow was out on his round.

He was the last postman to go that day. The big letters were done and the big bags were back on the van. Only the small bag was left, with one single thing in it.

It was not a letter.

It was flat, and brown, and very light. Lighter than a birthday card, almost. There was a stamp in one corner and an address in pencil across the front.

"That is a strange thing," said Mr. Barrow, to nobody at all. He said it quite seriously, the way you do about a job.

He got back on his bicycle and rode the last of the round slowly, because it was nearly time to go home. The last house on the whole round was number 41, and it was in his book.

He could not find number 41.

Not because it wasn't there. It was right at the end of the row, behind a gate and a hedge, with one light on in the kitchen.

But somebody had painted the front of the house a new colour, and the new colour was exactly the colour the number used to be.

So all that was left was a house. Number 41 had gone. Mr. Barrow looked at his book, and then at the hedge, and then at the book again.

He was just turning back for the depot when a voice came out from behind the gate.

"Are you looking for me?"

A small child in a dressing gown stood in the gap, holding the gate shut with one knee.

Mr. Barrow took off his hat. He felt strongly that this was the correct thing to do.

"Are you number 41?" he asked.

"No," said the child. "We're on the corner. The number's under the paint, and you can only see it in the rain."

"Then I believe this is yours," said Mr. Barrow, and held out the little brown thing.

The child did not take it. They looked at it for a long moment. "That's mine," they said. "I posted it this morning."

Mr. Barrow looked at the address, which was in very small writing, and belonged to the house they were both standing in front of.

"You posted it to yourself."

"Yes," said the child, and nodded, as if this were a perfectly ordinary thing that everybody did every day.

It was not a thing that everybody did every day. It was the very first letter the child had ever sent, of any kind, ever.

Mr. Barrow asked for a name and wrote it in his book, and asked them to sign for it, and the child signed very slowly with a long squiggle.

"I've never had a letter before," said the child. "I wanted to see if they really come."

And they had come. That was the whole surprise of the evening.

The postman had no more deliveries, so he sat down on the low wall by the gate, the child sat down beside him, and together they read the letter out loud under the street lamp.

It was not very long. It said: Dear me. I hope you are still nine, and that you still like the sea. I did it.

Mr. Barrow said it was a very good letter, and that he had delivered a great many worse ones.

Then he got back on his bicycle, said goodnight, and rode the half mile to the depot whistling, with an empty bag that felt about as light as the letter had been.

And the child stood in the doorway until his little red light went round the corner and was gone, and then went inside, and put the letter on the table, and read it again.

THE END.

One question for afterwards: If you could send one small thing to somebody far away, what would you put in the envelope?

Did you know? The first postage stamp in the world was the Penny Black, in 1840. It had no country written on it at all, because in those days there was only one country posting letters.
